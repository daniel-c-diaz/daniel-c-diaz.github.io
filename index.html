Previously LHC Physics Center Distinguished Researcher, Fermilab, 2023 & 2024. Postdoctoral scholar with Javier Duarte at UC San Diego. Ph.D., Florida State University, 2020.

Most of what the LHC produces is thrown away a millionth of a second after it happens. I work on the possibility that something important is in what we discard.

Every general-purpose search at the LHC assumes a new particle decays essentially the instant it is made. That assumption is built into the triggers, the reconstruction and the analyses. But if dark matter reaches the Standard Model through a narrow portal — as a great many models suggest — the coupling is feeble, and a feeble coupling means a long life. The particle survives long enough to cross part of the detector before it decays, and it leaves a signature that our instruments are specifically configured to reject as noise.

My work is about closing that gap: designing searches for decays that happen centimetres, metres, or tens of metres from the collision point, and building the real-time algorithms that keep those events in the first place.

What I work on

Searches for long-lived particles

I led a CMS search for particles decaying to displaced jets inside the tracker, which set some of the first limits on Higgs-portal long-lived particles below 20 GeV. Then, with collaborators, I helped develop a very different approach: treating the endcap muon detectors as a sampling calorimeter, where a decay in the steel produces a dense shower that essentially nothing in the Standard Model imitates. That opened up lifetimes where a particle travels hundreds of metres — a regime no tracker-based search can reach.

Machine learning inside the trigger

The LHC collides protons forty million times a second and keeps a few hundred of those events. The decision is made in microseconds by custom electronics, and what it rejects is gone permanently. I build and deploy neural networks at that layer — displaced-jet taggers and learned missing-energy algorithms running on FPGAs with hls4ml — so that the signatures I am looking for survive long enough for anyone to study them.

A new dataset for the low-mass frontier

Most recently I have led an analysis of the CMS B-parking dataset: billions of recorded b-hadron decays, collected for an entirely different purpose. Because b-hadrons are produced copiously and decay to light states, that dataset reaches dark-sector mediators too light for any standard trigger to have kept. I built the analysis framework and the background estimate, and supervised the students who carried it through.

Research computing and FAIR practice

At SDSC I work on the large-scale AI and computing infrastructure that data-intensive science runs on, including the National Research Platform. It connects back to the physics through one conviction: a trained model is a scientific result and ought to behave like one. Through FAIR4HEP I helped write the community standards for making AI models in high energy physics findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable.

Students

Eighteen students have worked with me — graduate, undergraduate, postbaccalaureate and high school. They built the taggers, ran the analyses and produced results that appear in CMS publications; the B-parking search in particular was carried by two graduate students and an undergraduate I supervised.

I teach at the Fermilab Hands-On Advanced Tutorial series on triggers, machine learning and jet substructure, and at the CMS Data Analysis School. I chair the US CMS Mentorship Committee and I am always glad to hear from students looking for a way into this field.
